QeMCMC · Correlated Network Risk

When failures are correlated, which node do you protect?

A sampler for any system whose risk is dominated by pairwise correlated failure. One Hamiltonian, one algorithm, one readout — the domain only changes what the couplings mean.

The problem

Risk models usually assume nodes fail independently. Real networks do not: one failure propagates to whatever it is coupled to. Modelling the full correlated failure distribution over n nodes costs 2n configurations — intractable past a few dozen nodes. And the quantity a decision-maker needs is a distribution (how likely is a catastrophic multi-node cascade at this stress level?), not a single worst-case configuration.

One encoding covers all of it

Binary node state + pairwise couplings + a longitudinal field is an Ising model: H(z) = ½·zᵀJz + hᵀz, with P(z) ∝ e−βH(z). Nothing in that structure is domain-specific. If your system has nodes that can fail, edges along which failure propagates, and a stress level, it is this model — only the meaning of Jij changes.

Why classical sampling is not enough

The decision quantity is the susceptibility χij = β·Cov(zi, zj): by fluctuation–dissipation it is exactly ∂⟨zi⟩/∂hj, so its column mean is the marginal value of intervening at node j. But near a tipping point the distribution has two deep basins. Single-spin Metropolis sticks in whichever one it started in, so its covariance — and the whole intervention ranking built on it — collapses.

What QeMCMC does

A quantum processor proposes moves; the classical Metropolis–Hastings test accepts them, so the chain still converges to the exact Boltzmann distribution — regardless of circuit noise, since the chain is classical between steps. Evolving under (1−γ)·α·Hprob + γ·ΣjXj gives proposals far away in Hamming distance but close in energy: the chain tunnels between basins instead of diffusing between them.

How a shock enters

Across the classical and quantum literature a shock enters an Ising risk model in exactly three ways, and all three are diagonal — they keep the target a classical Boltzmann distribution, which is what makes the Metropolis acceptance test and the exact certification possible at all.

  1. Field kick. Δhi on the exposed nodes — a storm front, a demand surge, a funding squeeze. The equilibrium shifts; the cascade is the response.
  2. Structure change. A coupling Jij appears, strengthens or breaks — a link severed, an exposure grown.
  3. Seed pinning. One node forced into the failed state as a boundary condition; the network equilibrates around it.

An intervention is the same operation with the opposite sign: hj → hj − ω. That is why a bank bailout and a road widening are literally the same edit to the same object, and why the sampler never has to know which domain it is in.

What we claim — and what we do not

Given a large enough step budget, both samplers converge. The result is a time-to-accuracy gap: how many iterations each one needs before its intervention ranking matches the exact answer. The quantum arm here is an exact statevector simulation, so no wall-clock speedup is claimed — per-step cost is reported alongside every result, and ground truth comes from exact 2n enumeration, in your browser. The advantage is also empirical and small-scale: Layden et al. demonstrated it on 27 qubits at n = 10.

Where the same model applies

Each of these is the same Hamiltonian with a different reading of Jij. We built two of them; the rest need a coupling matrix, not new algorithm.

A domain is a good fit when three things hold: correlations dominate (if failures really are independent, a quantum sampler adds nothing); tail risk is the decision variable; and the coupling graph is sparse enough to keep proposal circuits shallow.
